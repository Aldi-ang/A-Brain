Research Brief for Consensus

Why did Java rajangan tobacco from the 2026 El Niño season lose its aroma and smoothness, and what can growers and traders do about it?

Prepared 5 October 2026 for a tobacco grading and trading business in Prambanan, Central Java (partner of Gudang Garam since 1988). Built from two earlier research pages, a fact-check of a Gemini chat, and the owner's field knowledge. Language: English, because Consensus searches English-language papers. Indonesian trade terms are explained in the glossary (section 10).

How this brief is meant to be used
  1. Consensus does the literature research for the open questions in section 7, one prompt at a time from section 8.
  2. For every prompt, Consensus returns a table in the format of section 9, with its citations.
  3. The owner pastes the Consensus answers back to Claude, which turns them into visual pages and interactive graphs (plan in section 9).

Confidence labels used below: VERIFIED read in a source · LIKELY inferred from research · FIELD the owner's own experience · UNVERIFIED no source found yet.

1. The product and the process

Product: Javanese tembakau rajangan: sun-cured tobacco whose leaves are shredded before drying. Not krosok (whole-leaf cured). The owner trades grade B from Prambanan/Klaten (lower-middle leaves, light and mildly sweet; the draft Indonesian standard does not require grade B to be "oily").

StepLocal nameWhat happens
1. HarvestpetikLeaves picked by position, bottom first. Ideal when 60–70% of the leaf surface has turned yellow in the field.
2. Yellowingimbon / pemeramanLeaves stacked in the shade so they yellow evenly while still alive. Normally about 3 days for grade B; in 2026 it took about 6.
3. ShreddingrajangYellowed leaves cut into fine strands.
4. Sun-dryingjemur di widigStrands spread on bamboo racks, turned about every 2 hours.
5. ConditioningembunDried strands take in night dew so they become supple before packing.
6. StoragekeranjangPacked in baskets; flavour mellows with ageing.

3. What the owner observes (field knowledge)

  • This year's tobacco is mostly "jedug": a direct, wall-like hit, with none of the usual "halus" (smooth, slowly rising) character. Aroma is weak. Weleri tobacco lost its aroma too. FIELD
  • Normal regional character: Boyolali hits the nose directly ("njedug") and is not aromatic; Temanggung is smooth with a slowly rising impact and is aromatic; Muntilan is aromatic. FIELD
  • Sugar makes tobacco smoke hotter and less "anteb" (less impact). FIELD
  • Many leaves look a good yellow or green but taste bitter. FIELD
  • Yellowing (imbon) for grade B went from about 3 days to about 6. Imbon time differs by area, tobacco type, seed variety and grade. In Prambanan, upper leaves ("tenggok", "protol") take longer than lower leaves ("rewosan", "daun bawah"). FIELD
  • Farmers who use a lot of urea get leaves that will not ripen (yellow) in the imbon. FIELD
  • Wet-leaf sellers pick leaves before they have aged on the plant, to sell fast at a high price. Such leaves often do not ripen or yellow in the imbon, and the tobacco is less aromatic and less impactful. FIELD
  • "Keluwasen": expired leaves, caused by an imbon that is too long. "Ngrapak": shredded tobacco that feels crisp when touched. FIELD

4. The working explanation so far

Dry heat helped the harvest and drying (bright colour, low moisture, higher prices) but hit earlier, during leaf growth (June–August). The leaf formed less sugar, nicotine and nitrogen became concentrated, and leaves were thicker and slower to yellow. A longer imbon then burned more sugar through respiration. Low sugar with high nicotine gives an alkaline, "jedug" smoke. Heat at the tongue comes from over-dried ("ngrapak") strands and from sugar sprayed on by some sellers. Aroma suffers from low sugar (fewer browning-reaction products), early picking and an imbon that is unfinished or too long.

6. What the research has to deliver

Numbers, not only directions. For every question in section 7 the owner wants to know: which way the effect goes, how big it is, in which tobacco type and country, and how strong the evidence is. Sun-cured, air-cured and oriental tobacco studies are the closest match to rajangan; flue-cured (Virginia) studies are useful but should be labelled as such. Indonesian studies (Balittas, Indonesian university journals) are especially welcome.

7. Open research questions

  1. How much does drought during rapid growth change reducing sugar, nicotine and total nitrogen, by severity and growth stage?
  2. How does high air temperature during leaf maturation change sugar, nicotine and aroma compounds?
  3. Dose-response: nitrogen rate (urea) versus maturity timing, yellowing time, nicotine and sugar.
  4. What happens to yellowing time, starch, protein, sugar and aroma when leaves are picked before they are mature?
  5. How do leaf position and leaf thickness affect nicotine, sugar and yellowing time?
  6. How fast does sugar fall per day of yellowing, at which temperatures, and where is the quality peak?
  7. Which relative humidity during yellowing prevents "green-fixed" leaves, and what does low humidity do?
  8. Does an over-long yellowing ("keluwasen") raise protein breakdown, free amino acids and ammonia, and smoke irritation?
  9. How does drying speed of shredded tobacco ("ngrapak") change brittleness, burn rate, smoke temperature and harshness?
  10. Is the sugar-to-nicotine ratio linked to perceived harshness, impact and smoothness, and through smoke pH?
  11. Does added sugar (casing) raise combustion temperature or tongue bite, and lower perceived nicotine impact?
  12. How do drought, heat, wind and rain change trichome density and leaf-surface exudates, and how do those relate to aroma?
  13. What do Indonesian studies say about pemeraman (yellowing) duration and rajangan quality?

9. Answer format to request

Add this to the end of every prompt (or send it as a follow-up):

Present the results as a table with these columns: Study (author, year) | Country | Tobacco type | Treatment or condition | Outcome measured | Direction (increase / decrease / no effect) | Size of effect (% or units) | Design and sample size | Link. Then give a three-sentence bottom line and rate how strong the evidence is (strong / moderate / weak).

What Claude builds from the answers: one interactive page per topic group (plant, curing, drying, smoke), a dose-response graph for nitrogen, a sugar-versus-days curve with a slider for yellowing, a sugar/nicotine scatter, and an evidence map where every claim on this brief is plotted by effect direction and evidence strength. Charts will only use numbers that come with a citation.
